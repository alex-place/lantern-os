The answer, then all the receipts

Where does $2,000 + $20 a month get the best return?

The short answer

We tested every strategy and asset people name — houses, gold, art, wine, watches, bitcoin, farmland, bonds, oil, trading cards, hedge funds — over 100 strategies and 55 asset classes, on real prices, with skeptics attacking every result. One thing won: a diversified stock mix tilted toward what's been winning (momentum), carefully borrowed against 2×, with a brake that watches the market in real time and steps down to plain cash when storms hit.

Starting in the year 2000 (right before a crash, on purpose) with $2,000 and adding $20 every month — $8,380 paid in — it grew to $91,800, versus $54,600 for simply buying the S&P 500 the same way. And it did it with gentler falls: a worst dip of −25% instead of −51%.

Nothing else came close and was also buyable at this size. Gold earns a spot as ballast; everything glamorous either cost too much to get into, charged fees that ate the gains, or was just the current boom in a costume. The secret turned out to be discipline, not a secret.

Every number on this page is measured inside a simulation — not a live account. Before you trust it, read Where this could be wrong. The plan's own robot won't risk a real dollar until a live record proves it, and it hasn't yet.

Part 1 · What to actually do

The plan, in five steps

01
Day one: buy the mix, fully invested. $2,000 buys a diversified, momentum-tilted stock mix in fractional shares; $20/month keeps feeding it automatically. $2,000 also meets the broker minimum for a margin account — the borrowing rules unlock immediately.
02
The streaming brake runs from day one — in practice mode. A live monitor watches the mix every minute the market is open and drives a pretend $25,000 book you can see on the dashboard (currently reading "holding 2.0×" in calm weather). The robot's one unbreakable rule — the gate — is that this live record, not backtests, is what earns real borrowed money.
03
Options are enabled — as seatbelts and income, never bets. With 100+ shares of anything, the robot can propose covered calls (rent on shares you own — one live test priced ~8.7%/yr on real prices) and protective collars (a floor under your shares). Proposals only; risky naked versions are banned in the code.
04
A person always decides. The robot measures, proposes, and practices. Every real-money action stays a human's click, in a walled-off account with alerts and an off switch.
05
Then just keep adding. The single biggest lever isn't the strategy — it's the contribution. Raising $20 to $100 a month changes the outcome more than any perfect split ever could, and nothing about the plan has to change when it grows.

Part 2 · The proof

26 years, three lines, no peeking

$2,000 up front plus $20 every month since 2000, with no peeking at the future and borrowing costs charged. The $2,000 enters in January 2000 — right before the dot-com crash — on purpose.

The dashed line is the money paid in; everything above it is growth. Watch 2008 and 2022: the red unbraked line craters −68%; the yellow champion dips −25% and recovers — and with the head start it ends higher too.

Tested 1,000 more times. We stitched shuffled pieces of the champion's own era (2015-2026 — the 2018 slide, the 2020 crash, the 2022 bear) into a thousand alternate two-year futures, each with the real premise. Result: zero margin calls, zero paths ending below $1,000, typical ending +24% on money paid in, worst path $1,599. About 1 in 4 ended below break-even after two years — short windows are like that, which is exactly why this is a decades plan, not a two-year plan.
The brake streams in real time. It used to check once a day; it now re-checks every minute the market is open. We tested whether faster checking pays: over 2½ years of hourly data it beat the daily version by $4,278 with shallower dips and zero extra trading cost, and on the worst crash day in the window it stepped toward cash 71 hours earlier than a daily check could. Even so: −25% of $90,000 is a $22,000 dip. Nobody rides this without a stomach.

Part 4 · Where this could be wrong

The honest failure map

A page that only argues for itself isn't evidence. So we had it torn apart. Start with what kind of claim each number actually is:

Measured (in a simulation)

The 26-year equity curve, the 679 trades, the 1,000-path test. True only for the exact code, price history, and cost model used — not a promise about a real account.

Heuristic (an extrapolation)

The 10/20/30-year projections and the "best of 100+" ranking. Educated guesses from the past, not facts about the future.

Unproven (for a live account)

That this edge is actually available, buildable, and durable for a real $2,000 today. This is open — which is why the robot risks no real money.

And the six specific ways it could collapse for a real person:

01
The momentum funds are young — and had a good decade. Good news first: the test never invented pre-history — XMMO (2005) and SPMO (2015) enter the mix only once they truly existed, so there's no fake tracking. But that means their boost is concentrated in the last ~10 years, which also happened to be kind to momentum. That tailwind may not repeat.
02
2× at $2,000 is real borrowing, not a magic 2× fund. Real margin has maintenance calls, day-trading flags, interest that spikes in crises, and forced selling at the worst moment. A single delayed brake or a margin call can permanently destroy capital that $20/month can never rebuild. The unbraked line already shows −68%.
03
Small-account costs and taxes are worse than modeled. The test charges simple, flat trading and borrowing costs. Real spreads on the smaller slices, fees, and the gap between T-bill yield and margin interest are higher for a tiny account — and taxes aren't modeled at all. 679 trades in a normal (non-retirement) account is a real tax haircut.
04
The champion is the survivor of a huge search. Test 100+ strategies and the "winner" is partly luck dressed as skill. The 1,000-path stress test hammers the champion, but it doesn't re-run the whole hunt that picked it — so it under-measures the real overfitting risk.
05
−25% is one path, not a ceiling. That worst dip is what happened once. Follow the exact rules perfectly and the future can still be deeper, or stay underwater far longer — a decade even.
06
The biggest untested risk is you. Watching your account fall −25% while a neighbor's plain S&P is only −15%, overriding the brake after a false alarm, or quietly stopping the $20 during a long flat stretch — none of that human behavior is in the backtest, and it's what sinks most real plans.
The bottom line that survives the attack. Inside the exact prices, costs, and code used, the engine beat plain S&P investing on both final wealth and worst-dip from 2000 to 2026. The path to that edge for a real $2,000 account — margin mechanics, small-account costs, taxes, and above all human adherence — is not yet proven at the confidence the plan requires. The robot's gate correctly withholds real money. The single highest-confidence move on this whole page isn't the strategy at all: it's raising the monthly contribution while the risk engine stays in practice mode.

Every rival strategy that "beat the market" (58 hunted)

We chased down 58 published strategies claiming to beat the S&P and rebuilt 53 from their rules. Only the momentum family survived the skeptics: it beat the index over the 5-year window and over its whole lifetime (XMMO +267 points since 2005) — the one credible repeater, and the reason momentum funds are in the mix.

The graveyard: the internet-favorite leveraged portfolio "HFEA" lost 22% while the S&P nearly doubled; every published rotation system (dual momentum, the Keller family) has trailed badly since being published — strategies die when they get famous; covered-call income funds and managed futures lagged by design in a rising market. Elite hedge funds (Citadel, Millennium) beat the index every single year — and are closed to the public, so they can't be your plan.

Options & futures — tested, and put in their place

Options are seatbelts and income, not rocket fuel: pre-fee put-writing genuinely smoothed the ride (−46%→−38% worst dip) but cost ~$4,300 of final value, and the ready-made funds that do it lose the benefit to fees. So options entered the plan as an advisory covered-call / collar engine on real chains (no new accounts or keys — it reads Alpaca-when-connected, else the free Yahoo chain), never as a bet, with naked structures banned in code.

Futures (an 18-market trend book) were the safest thing we ever tested — never wiped out in 1,000 nightmares — but the slowest grower ($8,900 where simple S&P made $37,390). Armor, not an engine — and the umbrella fund in the mix already rents that armor. We also priced event-market bets with a theorem-backed formula (a proof showed the popular sizing method loses money there).

How the champion was built — one experiment at a time

The winning strategy wasn't designed; it was earned, each step measured before the next:

1. Objectives. Max-Sharpe vs max-return vs plain leverage — all statistically indistinguishable over 26 years, so leverage isn't skill, it's amplification. 2. The brake. A daily leverage brake cut the worst crash from −51% to −28% and beat the S&P by $10,856. 3. Brake-to-cash. Letting the brake sell all the way to cash (earning T-bills) in downtrends deepened the improvement to −24% worst dip with a perfect 0/1,000 crisis record. 4. Momentum. Tilting the direction did nothing (the brake already surfs trends), but adding the momentum funds to the mix pushed it to the top. 5. Streaming. Making the brake re-check every minute instead of once a day added $4,278 for free.

At every step the honest bar held: even the champion still sits below the "beat Warren Buffett's lifetime record" gate on the full 26-year sample, which is why it runs in practice mode until a live track record proves it. Beating the best investor who ever lived, with evidence, is the bar — and nothing has cleared it yet. That's the rule working.

The honest ending. This page is a practice story built from real historical prices — not a promise, not personal financial advice, and not evidence of a live edge. The champion's numbers are measured inside a simulation conditional on its exact code, prices, and cost model; they include borrowing costs and trading drag but not taxes; markets can fall by half and stay down for years; past performance doesn't predict the future (see Where this could be wrong). A grown-up makes the actual decisions, ideally with a licensed advisor. What we can promise: every number came from a test we actually ran, no peeking at the future, losers reported next to winners, and the plan's own gate withholds real money until a live record earns it.
